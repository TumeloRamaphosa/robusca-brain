# One engine, five audiences

**Prepared:** 25 Aug 2026

The layer diagram is a content strategy as well as a business structure. Each
layer is a different audience with a different reason to care, and one engine
serves all five — because in a chain where each layer is the next layer's
customer, **content aimed at one layer recruits the layer below it.**

That is the leverage. Most companies run one audience. This structure lets one
pillar do two jobs.

---

## The five audiences

| Layer | Who reads | What they want | What convinces them |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Global Companies** | Investors, cloud providers, OEMs, governments, universities | Is this real, and is it big? | Verified traction, correct licences, sanctions-clean counterparties, sober numbers |
| **Studex Global Markets** | Trade partners, capital, institutions | Can they execute across borders? | Named relationships, documented process, regulatory literacy |
| **Super Agents** | The top 20–100 companies per country. Operations leads with procurement | Who is accountable when it gets it wrong? | The audit trail, the approval gate, a delivered client |
| **Agentic Rise** | 100–1,000 startups per country. Developers, engineers, studios, consultants | Is there work here, and will I get paid? | Real projects, clear standards, a published way in |
| **Arcade / Midnight Founders** | Gamers, programmers, creators, students, founders | Can I get somewhere from here? | Visible progression, real people who advanced |

### The two-job principle

A piece for Super Agents buyers about how managed agent delivery actually works
is simultaneously a recruitment document for Agentic Rise — because a developer
reading how the work is specified is reading a job description. Write once, land
twice, and say so in the brief.

The strongest version of this is publishing our standards. A document titled "how
we specify and review agent work" sells to a buyer *and* recruits a delivery
partner *and* filters out people who would do it badly.

---

## Priority

Do not run all five. Ranked by whether content actually moves the constraint:

**1. Super Agents — everything goes here first.**
The load-bearing layer. Every layer above supplies inputs to it and every layer
below supplies labour for it. If the middle does not work, the diagram is an org
chart for a company with no product. All five ICP profiles should be drawn from
this audience initially.

**2. Agentic Rise — second, and cheaper than expected.**
Largely a by-product of layer 1 content. The main original asset needed is a
published standard for how work is specified, reviewed and paid.

**3. Global Companies — only when the facts are straight.**
Do not produce investor-facing content until the risk register findings are
closed. A polished document containing an incorrect licence claim and a sanctioned
counterparty gets further into diligence before it fails, which is worse than
failing early.

**4. Arcade and Midnight Founders — later.**
These need a story of progression to tell, and there is no one to point at yet.
Content here before the first cohort is aspiration, and it reads as such.

**5. Global Markets — as needed, transactional.**
Relationship-led rather than content-led. Content follows a conversation here, it
does not start one.

Each carries a first-party element. Each is a real piece, not a topic.

### The three to write first

**Pillars 1 and 2** are the campaign spine. Publish the test, then take it
publicly and show the failures. Nobody else will.

**Pillar 6** is the sleeper. It is genuinely useful, almost nobody in the South
African market has written it accurately, it demonstrates regulatory literacy to
buyers, and it makes every competitor running cold email look reckless. It costs
us nothing because the analysis is already done.

**Pillar 9** is the one that will feel uncomfortable and will outperform.
Publishing a real internal failure is the least fakeable content available, and
it is currently sitting in `content/2026-06-14/` doing nothing.

Pillar 12 is worth noting for what it demonstrates: choosing not to do something
for a stated regulatory reason is a stronger signal to an institutional investor
than any capability claim, because it shows judgement rather than enthusiasm.

## Cadence, mapped

One pillar a week, three to five derivatives from each.

| Week | Pillar | Derivatives land in |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The ten questions | Mon, Wed, Fri social slots |
| 2 | Our own score | Mon, Wed, Fri |
| 3 | Cold email / what we do instead | Mon, Wed, Fri |
| 4 | Refresh the best performer with new data | Tue, Thu |

Twelve pillars is a quarter of publishing at one a week, all of it non-commodity,
none of it requiring a client to exist first. Then pillar 10 arrives, and it is
worth more than the other eleven combined.

## The rule for the posting calendar

The existing 24-hour calendar has nine slots a day. **Fill them from derivatives
only, and leave them empty otherwise.**

An empty slot costs nothing. A commodity post costs attention that has to be
earned back, and per [04-PROVENANCE.md](04-PROVENANCE.md) it will not be indexed
anyway. Nine slots a day was designed for a different content economy than the
one that exists now.

Three good posts a week from a pillar that says something original will
outperform sixty-three generated captions, and it is less work.
